The Atelier Process

The Craft

From raw fiber to finished binding, every sheet and stitch is shaped by hand. This is how a book is born.

1
Chapter I

From Fiber to Sheet

  1. I

    Beating the Fiber

    Raw cotton linter arrives in compressed bales. We soak it overnight, then feed it through a Hollander beater where rotating blades separate individual fibers without cutting them. The beating time determines the paper's character: a short beat produces a coarse, textured sheet; an extended beat yields a smooth, translucent vellum. We test by holding a pinch of slurry to the light.

    → assets/process-beating.jpeg
  2. II

    Forming the Sheet

    A wooden mould and deckle are dipped into the vat of prepared pulp. The papermaker lifts the mould, shaking it gently to cross the fibers and create an even sheet. The deckle gives each sheet its signature rough, feathered edge that no machine can replicate. This motion, unchanged for centuries, is the heartbeat of the craft.

    → assets/process-forming.jpeg
  3. III

    Pressing & Couching

    Each wet sheet is couched onto a felt blanket and stacked into a post. The post goes into a screw press, where slow, even pressure expels water without crushing the fiber structure. We press in stages, increasing pressure gradually over hours. The felt leaves a subtle texture in the finished sheet, a whisper of the process preserved in the surface.

    → assets/process-pressing.jpeg
  4. IV

    Loft Drying

    Pressed sheets are peeled from the felts and hung on wooden poles in the drying loft. Air circulation does the work, not heat. The sheets dry slowly over two to three days, shrinking slightly and developing their final texture. A properly dried sheet rings when flicked at the corner. Only then is it ready for sizing, pressing flat, and cutting to dimension.

    → assets/process-drying.jpeg

Chapter II

The Binding

→ assets/binding-coptic.jpeg
Technique 01

Coptic Stitch

The oldest codex binding. Interlocking chain stitches connect signatures without adhesive, allowing the book to open completely flat. Each link is both structural and ornamental.

→ assets/binding-stab.jpeg
Technique 02

Japanese Stab

A side-sewn binding with geometric thread patterns visible on the spine. We use waxed linen and work four or five-hole patterns drawn from traditional Yotsume Toji and Kangxi designs.

→ assets/binding-case.jpeg
Technique 03

Case Binding

The classic hardcover. Signatures are sewn to tapes, the spine rounded and backed, then the text block is cased into cloth or leather-wrapped boards. Headbands are sewn by hand, not glued.

→ assets/binding-longstitch.jpeg
Technique 04

Long Stitch

Thread passes along the outside of the spine cover in long, visible runs. The binding becomes the decoration. We pair this with leather or heavy paper covers where the thread reads as a design element.

Chapter III

Materials

→ assets/material-cotton.jpeg
01

Cotton Linter

The foundation of every sheet. We source long-staple cotton linter from mills in the American South. Its cellulose fibers interlock during beating, producing a sheet that is strong, archival, and luminous when held to light.

→ assets/material-linen.jpeg
02

Linen Thread

Irish-spun, hand-waxed with beeswax for grip and longevity. Linen thread does not stretch or rot. We carry it in eighteen weights, from hairline sewing thread to heavy cord for coptic chain stitching.

→ assets/material-leather.jpeg
03

Goatskin Leather

Vegetable-tanned in small batches by a tannery in the English Midlands. The grain is tight, the paring thin and even. We use it for full bindings, half bindings, and spine panels. It darkens with age and develops a patina that is impossible to simulate.

→ assets/material-paste.jpeg
04

Handmade Paste Papers

We make our own decorative papers using wheat starch paste mixed with earth pigments. Each sheet is combed, swirled, or stippled by hand while the paste is still wet. No two sheets are alike. They line our endpapers and wrap our boards.
