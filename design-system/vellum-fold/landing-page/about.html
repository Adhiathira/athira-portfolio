Chapter I

The Beginning

In the autumn of 2008, in a converted stone barn on the edge of the Cotswolds, Eleanor Ashworth pulled a sheet of cotton paper from a vat of warm water and held it to the light. The fibres had locked together in a way she had not planned but immediately recognised as right. That sheet became the first page of a journal she bound by hand that same evening, stitching the signatures with linen thread under a single bare bulb. Deckle & Thread was not founded that night — it was discovered.

Eleanor had spent a decade restoring damaged books for institutional libraries — reattaching spines, consolidating foxed pages, rebuilding boards from scratch. The work taught her to read a book not as text but as an object: the grain direction of the paper, the swell of the spine, the way a well-made binding opens with a whisper rather than a crack. She began to wonder what a book would look like if it were made entirely from first principles — paper formed by hand, boards cut from raw millboard, covers wrapped in cloth woven on a local loom.

The answer was the first Deckle & Thread journal: sixty-four leaves of handmade cotton rag paper, Coptic-stitched so the spine lay flat, bound in indigo-dyed linen. She made twelve copies. They sold in a single afternoon at a craft market in Cirencester.

Chapter II

The Ethos

We believe a book is a room. Its covers are walls, its spine is a threshold, and its pages are the space within — a space shaped by the materials from which it is built. When those materials are handmade, the room has texture: the slight irregularity of deckle-edged paper, the visible stitch of a hand-sewn spine, the warmth of vegetable-tanned leather that deepens with age. These are not imperfections. They are evidence of presence — proof that a human being stood at the vat, held the mould, pulled the sheet.

"A machine-made book is information housed in a container. A handmade book is information that has become inseparable from its container."
Eleanor Ashworth, Founder

Our practice is deliberately slow. We do not optimise for volume. A single sheet of cotton paper takes forty-eight hours to form, couch, press, and dry. A bound journal passes through nine distinct stages over the course of two weeks. An artist's book — a one-off commission — can take three months from first conversation to final binding. This duration is not inefficiency; it is the time the materials require to become what they are meant to become.

We work with raw cotton linter, not pre-processed pulp. We size our papers with gelatine rendered from hides sourced at the same tannery that supplies our binding leather. We spin our own linen thread. These choices are not nostalgia — they are engineering decisions. Cotton linter produces a paper with a tensile strength that pulp cannot match. Gelatine sizing resists ink feathering for two centuries. Hand-spun thread, waxed with beeswax, creates a binding that flexes without snapping.

"Permanence is not a feature. It is the baseline. Everything we make is built to outlast the person who made it."
Deckle & Thread Workshop Principle

The book form is the most durable information technology ever devised. A codex made with sound materials and correct technique will remain legible for five hundred years. We take that inheritance seriously. Every journal, every artist's book, every commission that leaves this studio is built to that standard — not because our clients demand it, but because the craft does.

Chapter III

The Space

The studio occupies a long, low stone building that was once a dairy on a working farm. The south wall is almost entirely glass — a row of steel-framed windows installed in the 1940s that flood the workspace with the diffuse, even light that papermaking demands. The north wall holds the vat, the press, and the drying racks: the three stations around which every sheet of paper in the studio's history has orbited.

The binding room is a separate space at the eastern end, divided from the papermaking floor by a heavy curtain. It is quieter here, drier, deliberately still. The bench runs the full length of the room — six metres of seasoned oak, scarred and stained from fifteen years of knife cuts, glue spills, and the slow accretion of linen dust. Above the bench, shallow shelves hold the tools of the trade: bone folders, awls, spring dividers, paring knives, a collection of brass finishing tools inherited from a retired binder in Oxfordshire.

  • Hollander beater for cotton fibre preparation
  • Two hydraulic book presses, each rated to four tonnes
  • Temperature-controlled drying chamber for sized papers
  • Traditional plough and guillotine for edge trimming
  • Natural dye kitchen for hand-colouring papers and fabrics

Chapter IV

The Hands

Eleanor Ashworth

Founder & Master Bookbinder

Eleanor trained in conservation bookbinding at the Bodleian Library before founding Deckle & Thread. Her speciality is the Coptic stitch and its variations — she has developed three original binding structures that allow handmade paper to lie perfectly flat without mechanical scoring. She is responsible for all commissions involving leather and vellum, and teaches an annual masterclass in non-adhesive binding at the Royal College of Art.

James Holt

Head Papermaker

James came to papermaking through printmaking — he spent five years as an editioning printer at a private press in Somerset before realising he was more interested in the paper than the type. He trained under a Japanese washi master in Echizen and brought back techniques for embedding botanical inclusions into wet sheets. Every sheet of paper in the studio passes through his hands at least twice.

Suki Arden

Textile Specialist & Cloth Binder

Suki trained as a weaver before joining the studio in 2014. She sources, dyes, and prepares all the cloth used in the binding room — Belgian linen, Japanese silk, and a heavy cotton canvas she weaves herself on a floor loom installed in the north annex. Her natural dye work uses materials foraged within walking distance of the studio: oak galls for iron-black, weld for yellow, indigo grown in the kitchen garden.

Thomas Wray

Apprentice Binder & Gilder

Thomas is the studio's youngest member, currently in the second year of a four-year apprenticeship. He arrived with a degree in fine art and an obsession with gold leaf. He has quickly become the studio's specialist in titling and edge gilding — the delicate work of applying 23-carat gold to the fore-edge, head, and tail of a bound book. His ambition is to master the technique of fore-edge painting: the art of painting a scene on fanned pages that becomes invisible when the book is closed.
