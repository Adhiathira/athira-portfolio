The Process

Craft without shortcut

Every sheet of paper we make passes through eight hands before it leaves the studio. We think that's the minimum. This is what happens.

01

Sourcing the fibre

We use 100% cotton rag — not wood pulp. Cotton produces paper that lasts centuries without acid degradation. Our source is a single supplier in New South Wales: a family operation that has grown the same cultivar for forty years. We take what they can spare.

The difference between cotton and wood pulp is not a marketing claim. Cotton fibre is long and strong. Under magnification, wood pulp looks like a pile of matchsticks. Cotton looks like hair.

02

Beating and hydration

Cotton rag is torn and soaked for twelve hours, then beaten in a Hollander beater — a rotating drum with iron bars that breaks fibre without cutting it. The degree of beating determines surface quality. Longer beating produces smoother paper. We err toward shorter: we like texture.

The resulting pulp — called furnish — is 98% water. It is this suspension, held in a vat at body temperature, that becomes paper.

03

Sheet formation

A mould — a wooden frame with a woven wire screen — is dipped into the furnish and lifted in a single fluid motion. The papermaker rocks the mould to distribute fibre evenly; then the water drains. What remains on the screen is a wet sheet of paper, already formed but fragile.

The deckled edges of our papers — the ragged, uneven borders — are a direct consequence of this process, not an affectation. Water flowing under the deckle frame leaves a natural feathered edge that no machine can reproduce.

04

Pressing and drying

Wet sheets are pressed between felts at controlled pressure to remove water without distorting grain. Then they are hung — individually, on wooden dowels — to dry for a minimum of 24 hours in the studio. We do not use heat. The paper finds its own moisture equilibrium with the room's air.

Drying is where the surface character sets. A warm, humid day produces slightly softer paper than a cold, dry one. We log weather conditions for every batch.

05

The impression

For letterpress papers, the dry sheet is fed through a 1963 Vandercook No. 4 proof press. At 800 pounds per square inch, the type locks into the cotton surface. Not on it — into it. The depression left by the letter is called the bite, and it is what distinguishes letterpress from offset printing: you can feel where the letter has been.

We print only with polymer plates — no lead, no antimony. The plates are made from digital files, which means any letterform can be pressed. But we keep our own collection of wood type for studio work.
