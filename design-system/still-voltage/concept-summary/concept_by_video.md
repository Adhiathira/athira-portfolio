# Motion and Temporal Design — Bruno Simon

## Scroll Opening

There is no scroll. This is the first and most structurally significant motion decision on the site: the page does not respond to vertical gesture in any conventional way. The opening state is therefore the only state — the entire motion budget is concentrated into a single temporal event. On load, the Three.js canvas initializes before any HTML overlay appears, meaning the first thing a visitor witnesses is the WebGL context coming alive: the dark navy field resolving from black, the `×` grid crosses fading into visibility against the near-black ground, and then the 3D island scene materializing into the center of the composition. This is not a page loading — it is a world booting. The transition from black to navigable scene carries the weight that most sites distribute across multiple scroll-triggered sections.

## Hero Experience

The hero entrance is the 3D scene itself emerging into its idle state, and it governs the entire temporal register of the experience. The cherry blossom tree — the composition's tallest element, bleeding past the viewport top — is almost certainly animated from the start: petals falling, branches swaying in a slow ambient cycle that has no beginning or end. The monster truck and architectural props sit still by contrast, their geometric rigidity making the organic tree motion feel more alive by opposition. The "CLICK TO START" text and hand-drawn arrow likely fade or draw in after the scene has resolved, arriving as annotation rather than headline — a deliberate sequencing that ensures the 3D world precedes the instruction to enter it. The sound icon may pulse or shimmer as a secondary invitation. The overall effect is a held breath before action: everything in the scene is technically in motion (idle animations, ambient lighting flicker, perhaps subtle camera drift) while the composition itself reads as stillness.

## Section Transitions

Section transitions do not exist in the scroll-between-sections sense. The single transition this interface performs is the click — the moment a visitor activates the scene and the portfolio experience begins. What comes after the click is not a page transition but a scene transition: the camera presumably swoops down or presses forward into the 3D world, eliminating the overview perspective and placing the viewer inside the environment. This is a temporal rupture rather than a spatial scroll. The before-state (cinematic overview of the island) and the after-state (interactive first- or third-person navigation within the scene) are separated by a single user gesture, not by a series of scroll increments. The motion design at this threshold is consequently the highest-stakes animation decision on the entire site — it must communicate the shift from observer to participant without disorientation.

## Content Rhythm

Because the interface delivers no content through scroll-triggered reveals, there is no staggered entry sequence, no text sliding in from left, no cards fading up in sequence. The content rhythm is replaced entirely by the spatial rhythm of the 3D world itself: how far apart objects are placed in the scene, how quickly the ambient animations cycle, how the camera responds to mouse input. Each element in the scene — the truck's headlights, the neon ring's pulse, the falling petals — operates on its own loop with its own timing. The accumulated effect of multiple asynchronous cycles creates an impression of organic life rather than choreographed sequence. This is a fundamentally different temporal logic than the reveal-driven rhythm of scroll-based sites: rather than content entering the viewport in designed order, everything is always present and always moving at its own rate.

## Footer Approach

No footer appears. This is not an omission — it is the most compressed expression of the site's temporal philosophy. A footer implies narrative closure: the page has ended, here is where you leave. Bruno Simon's interface declines this grammar entirely. The experience ends when the user navigates away, not when the page runs out. If any closing state exists, it would be the exit from the 3D world back to the overview — a possible return to the title screen state that would reverse the opening camera motion and restore the island-from-above perspective. This reverse transition, if it exists, would function as the closest analog to a footer: a spatial beat that signals the experience is available to be re-entered, not concluded.

## Motion Principles

The motion philosophy here is best described as **ambient persistence**: animations do not begin and end in response to user events — they run continuously, cyclically, at low energy, creating a living backdrop against which the single high-energy event (the click-to-enter transition) reads with maximum contrast. This stands in opposition to both the snappy micro-interaction school (discrete, triggered, fast-resolved) and the languid scroll-cinema school (slow reveals timed to scroll velocity). Instead the timing is biological — the rhythm of a scene that exists independent of whether anyone is watching it. The Three.js render loop runs at frame rate regardless of user input; the ambient animations breathe on their own schedule. The only mechanical element is the instruction text, which is static and unmoving — a deliberate stillness that makes it readable against the living scene behind it. The cumulative motion signature of this interface is not a set of easing curves or duration values: it is the impression of entering a place that was already in motion before you arrived.
