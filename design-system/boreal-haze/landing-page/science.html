Aurora Geophysics

The Physics of Light From Space

How charged particles from the Sun paint the sky in teal, violet, and lime.

Solar Wind

400 km/s — The Trigger Velocity

The Sun constantly exhales a stream of charged particles — electrons and protons accelerated to hundreds of kilometres per second. This solar wind takes 1–3 days to travel from Sun to Earth, but in moments of intense activity — coronal mass ejections — dense plasma clouds reach Earth in as little as 18 hours.

"When the IMF Bz turns southward, Earth's magnetosphere tears open. Auroras follow within 30 minutes."

The critical variable is the Interplanetary Magnetic Field (IMF) Bz component. When Bz points northward, Earth's field deflects the solar wind. When Bz flips southward, magnetic reconnection occurs at the dayside magnetopause — field lines break and reconnect, allowing energetic particles to funnel into the polar cusps and cascade down into the upper atmosphere where aurora ignites.

Emission Spectra

Why Aurora Is These Colors

Oxygen at 558 nm — Green

Oxygen at 558nm — The Green of the Aurora Oval

Oxygen atoms at 100–150 km altitude emit the characteristic green that defines the aurora oval band. This is the most common aurora color and the one most visible to dark-adapted eyes. When an oxygen atom absorbs a high-energy electron from the solar wind cascade, it enters an excited state. As it relaxes back to ground state, it releases a photon at precisely 557.7 nanometres — a vivid green-teal that fills the lower aurora bands. This altitude range is prime because the atmosphere is dense enough for frequent electron-oxygen collisions yet thin enough that emitted photons escape upward before being reabsorbed.

Oxygen at 630 nm — Red

Oxygen at 630nm — The Red Crown

At 200–300 km altitude, lower-density oxygen produces deep red above the green bands. The excited oxygen atoms at this height take much longer to emit — up to two minutes between excitation and photon release. Only during intense G3+ storms does sufficient particle energy reach this altitude to produce visible red aurora. The red crown appears above the green oval like a faint crimson halo — a reliable indicator that a major event is underway.

Nitrogen at 391 nm — Violet

Nitrogen at 391nm — The Violet Fringe

Ionized molecular nitrogen creates the blue-violet edge colors along the lower border of aurora curtains. Most intense during major CME events, nitrogen emission at 391.4 nm gives aurora its distinctive purple-blue fringe. Unlike oxygen, nitrogen molecules emit when directly ionized — they do not need to relax through a metastable state — so the response is nearly instantaneous with particle flux.

How Spectra Forecasts

From Sun to Prediction in Under 3 Hours

01

Solar Event Detection

ACE and DSCOVR satellites at the L1 Lagrange point — 1.5 million km sunward from Earth — detect incoming CMEs 30–60 minutes before Earth impact. Real-time solar wind speed, density, and magnetic field orientation data arrive at NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center within seconds of measurement.

02

Real-time Solar Wind Ingest

NOAA SWPC feeds velocity, density, and IMF Bz data to Spectra servers at 1-minute cadence. The Bz component is the critical variable — southward orientation triggers reconnection events. Spectra processes each feed update through its ensemble model pipeline within 4 seconds of receipt.

03

Magnetometer Analysis

48 ground stations capture the onset of geomagnetic disturbance and validate satellite predictions with real-world measurements. When magnetometers detect sudden commencement — the sharp initial pulse of a geomagnetic storm — Spectra's alert tier escalates immediately, independent of satellite prediction lead time.

04

Kp Forecast Output

Spectra's ensemble model outputs Kp forecasts at 15-minute intervals with probability distributions over G1–G5 storm classes. Each forecast includes a confidence band, peak Kp estimate, and aurora visibility map showing the geomagnetic latitude boundary where aurora is likely to be seen given current conditions.
