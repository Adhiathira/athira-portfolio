# Boreal Haze — Design Concept by Image

## Luminous Gradient Mesh

Boreal Haze is an Aurora/Gradient Glow design system built around the physics of light emerging from darkness. The foundational visual premise is a luminous gradient mesh — a layered field of overlapping radial gradients rendered against a near-black canvas, each gradient pool contributing to a continuously shifting chromatic field that reads as both technical and atmospheric. Unlike gradient systems that apply color to surfaces, this system treats light itself as the primary material: surfaces are defined not by fill but by the degree to which they permit or amplify the underlying luminance. The result is a visual register that belongs to deep-tech interfaces, generative art platforms, and premium SaaS products built around data with a strong aesthetic position.

## Aurora Borealis Chromatic Bands

The chromatic logic of the system follows the spectral structure of aurora borealis chromatic bands — not as decorative metaphor but as a governing organizational principle. The palette moves in a spectral palette progression from deep midnight (#161d25) through aurora teal (#21f3b6), violet (#9248e4), and electric lime (#e2ff65), each band occupying a distinct spatial and hierarchical role. Midnight is the page field — total void from which all other elements emerge. Aurora teal anchors interactive and primary action surfaces, carrying maximum luminous intensity in the darkest zones. Violet defines secondary structure and transitional states. Lime appears sparingly as an accent at highest contrast moments — a spectral outlier that punctuates rather than dominates. The band logic means that reading the page from top to bottom replicates the experience of watching real aurora shift through its spectral sequence.

## Glow Diffusion — Shadows That Illuminate Rather Than Darken

The interaction between light and dark in this system inverts conventional shadow logic. Standard design systems use shadows to add darkness beneath objects, simulating overhead light sources. Boreal Haze uses glow diffusion — shadows that illuminate rather than darken. Every elevated surface casts a luminous halo outward: a card floating above the void does not have a grey drop-shadow but a soft teal or violet radial emission that brightens the surrounding dark field. Text set in near-black environments carries a text-shadow in the glyph's own accent color at very low opacity, making type appear to emit rather than absorb. This physics inversion is the system's core surface quality: the page is not lit from above but illuminated from within.

## Spectral Palette Progression

The four-stop spectral palette progression (deep midnight → aurora teal → violet → lime) functions as a systematic hierarchy rather than a mood palette. Deep midnight (#161d25) fills the viewport canvas and section backgrounds — it is not black but a near-black with enough blue-shift to read as space rather than ink. Aurora teal (#21f3b6) carries the highest frequency luminance and is reserved for primary interactive affordances: CTA buttons, active states, progress indicators, and hover halos. Violet (#9248e4) occupies the mid-register — section headers, secondary accents, decorative gradient bands — providing tonal variety without competing with teal's role as the action color. Lime (#e2ff65) appears at moments of emphasis or completion — confirmation states, pull-quotes, high-contrast badges — giving the palette an unexpected frequency that signals event without alarm. The progression is deliberately spectral rather than complementary; harmony emerges from the physics of emission spectra, not color wheel geometry.

## Atmospheric Depth Through Light Physics

Spatial depth in this system is constructed through atmospheric depth through light physics rather than geometric perspective or elevation tokens. Near elements carry higher luminance intensity and sharper glow definition. Far elements diminish into the midnight field — their glow diffuses until the element and background become nearly indistinct. This behavior mirrors how aurora bands appear in the atmosphere: nearby bands are vivid and structured, distant bands dissolve into the sky's general luminosity. Container hierarchy is communicated entirely through glow radius and intensity: top-level cards carry a 0-24px teal box-shadow at 40% opacity; nested elements carry a 0-8px violet glow at 20%; background decorative shapes are visible only as subtle luminance variation against the void, with no defined edge — pure atmospheric depth.

## Floating Glass Surfaces on Dark Void

Component surfaces use floating glass surfaces on dark void — semi-transparent panels that reveal the gradient mesh behind them while maintaining enough contrast for content readability. Cards are rendered with background: rgba(255,255,255,0.04) — nearly invisible as white but carrying enough opacity to read as a plane — with a 1px border at rgba(255,255,255,0.08) to define the glass edge. The glass quality is enhanced by a backdrop-filter: blur(12px) saturate(180%) that intensifies the underlying gradient through the panel, making each card appear to concentrate the aurora light behind it. This surface language positions the system aesthetically between hard-edged UI and atmospheric visualization: structured enough for interaction design, immersive enough for a creative or data-rich product environment.
