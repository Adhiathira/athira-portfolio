Methodology

The Typographic
Argument.

"Weight is not style. Weight is hierarchy."

Type selection begins with argument, not taste.

Every typeface carries a set of inherited rhetorical positions — about authority, about speed of reading, about the relationship between reader and page. Our process begins not with visual preference but with an audit of what a typeface argues before a word is set. We read historical specimens, trace the contexts in which a face was deployed, and identify the weight registers it was designed to occupy.

Weight register is not a feature list. It is the grammar of emphasis — the way a publication builds hierarchy without resorting to colour, rule, or ornament. A face with a strong 900-weight and a legible 300-weight gives a designer two instruments: one for structure, one for reading field. The distance between those instruments is the measure of typographic range.

Editorial logic, finally, is the discipline of restraint. Not every hierarchy needs to be stated. Some must be implied — by the ratio of display mass to running text, by the depth of the leading, by the silence of the margin. Our methodology is a record of decisions made, not a recipe for decisions to come.

Four principles
1 Display mass as structure

Display type at maximum weight is not decoration — it is architecture. The 900-weight headline defines the vertical anchor of a spread, establishes the primary reading direction, and signals to the reader what the publication considers most important. A studio that treats display mass as ornament misunderstands the instrument.

2 Low-mass body as reading field

Body type at weight 300 creates a field of low visual resistance — a continuous surface the eye can traverse without interruption. The lightness is not weakness; it is hospitality. The reader enters a light-weight body text with less effort, reads further, retains more. Every extra unit of body weight is friction borrowed against readability.

3 Hairline rules as the only ornament

A 0.5-point rule is not decoration — it is a joint. It separates without interrupting, organises without asserting, concludes without summarising. Every ornament that is not a hairline rule is a question that must be answered: what argument does this element make that type alone could not? If there is no answer, remove the element.

4 Negative tracking at display scale

As type increases in size, the optical space between letterforms expands and the words begin to separate — loosening the headline into a list of shapes rather than a reading unit. Negative tracking at display scale is a correction, not a stylistic gesture. The degree of correction is determined by the face, the weight, and the optical size at which the type will be read. There is no universal formula; there is only careful looking.

Method — Three steps

01

Source material selection

Before a typeface enters the composition, we compile a dossier of its historical deployment — the publications it was designed for, the printers who first set it, the editors who adopted it and the reasons they gave. A typeface without a history is an instrument without a repertoire. We select type with a known argument, and we verify that argument against the publication's editorial position before committing to a face.

02

Weight register audit

We set the same paragraph of editorial text in every available weight of the chosen face — from ultralight through black — and examine the weight distribution across the hierarchy of a typical spread: headline, standfirst, body, caption, folio. The audit reveals whether a face has the range the publication requires, or whether it will collapse at one end of the hierarchy and distort at the other.

03

Composition proof

The composition proof is a full spread, set in print dimensions on the actual stock, with all hierarchy levels active. We read it in natural light, at arm's length, and in bad light at reduced scale — the three conditions under which publications are actually read. Only when the proof holds across all three conditions does the face enter the final system. Every proof that fails reveals something the audit did not.
