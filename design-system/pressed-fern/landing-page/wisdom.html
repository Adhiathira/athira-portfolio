Cultivation Guides

Knowledge passed
hand to hand.

Growing guides assembled from three generations of seed stewards. Not instructions — observations. The kind of knowledge written in soil, not spreadsheets.

Core Principles

The discipline of saving seed.

Seed-saving is not a technique. It is a discipline of observation — the practice of noticing what survives, what thrives, what holds its character across generations. Every steward learns it differently. These principles are what the archive has learned from all of them.

01

Grow to know

You cannot save seed from a variety you do not understand. Grow it first for eating. Then grow it again to learn its full expression — what it does in different soils and weathers.

02

Select for the variety

Always select from the plant that most faithfully expresses the variety's original character — not the largest, not the earliest, not the easiest. The truest.

03

Prevent isolation drift

Cross-pollinating species (brassicas, cucumbers, corn, beets) must be isolated by distance or timing. The minimum distances are not conservative suggestions — they are structural requirements.

04

Document every season

A seed without provenance is an orphan. Note the growing season, the soil conditions, the harvest date, any unusual behaviour. This is the metadata the archive needs to pass the seed forward with integrity.

Technique Guides

Method by family.

Wet Process

Tomatoes & Cucumbers

Ferment seed-gel for 48–72 hours in a small jar with water. The viable seeds sink; the non-viable float with the gel. Pour off the floating material, rinse the sunk seeds three times, dry on ceramic plates for 5–7 days. Never dry on paper towels — the seeds adhere and tear.

Varieties: Brandywine, Mortgage Lifter, Green Zebra, Boothby's Blonde
Dry Process

Beans, Peas & Corn

Leave pods on the plant until completely dry. In wet climates, pull the whole plant and hang it upside-down indoors. Thresh beans by placing dry pods in a cloth sack and striking against a hard surface. Winnow in front of a fan to separate seed from chaff. Store only fully dry seed — any moisture risks mould.

Varieties: Dragon Tongue, Champion of England, Fish Pepper
Umbellifer

Carrots & Parsnips

Biennial: plants must overwinter to flower. Protect taproots from hard frost in cold regions. Harvest seed umbels when brown and beginning to shatter. Bag umbels before fully dry to catch early-shattering seed.

Cucurbit

Squash & Pumpkins

Leave the fruit on the vine well past eating stage — 4–6 weeks longer. The seed continues to mature after the fruit is ripe to eat. Wash, dry for 3 weeks minimum. Varieties within the same species cross freely.

Allium

Onions & Leeks

Biennial cross-pollinators. Significant isolation distances required (1km minimum for pure seed). Cage over individual plants with bumble bees if space is limited. Seed shatters quickly — monitor daily at maturity.

Long-term Storage

A seed is only good
as its storage.

Most heirloom seeds will store 3–5 years under cool, dry, dark conditions. With controlled humidity and temperature, viability extends to a decade or longer. The archive stores critical varieties at 35–40°F in sealed borosilicate glass with silica desiccant.

Temperature
35–40°F

A domestic refrigerator (not freezer) maintains this range. Place seeds in the lowest-humidity zone — usually the middle or bottom shelf.

Humidity
Below 8%

Silica desiccant sachets, replaced when the indicator turns pink. Sealed glass is preferable to sealed plastic — it doesn't off-gas and maintains a true dry environment.

Viability test
Annual

Germinate 10 seeds on damp paper towel at room temperature. Count sprouts at 5 days and 10 days. Below 60% germination rate: grow out urgently. Below 30%: contact the archive for backup stock.
