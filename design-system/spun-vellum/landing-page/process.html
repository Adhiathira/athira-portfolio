How We Work

The Process

Four stages. No shortcuts.

01

Brief & Consultation

Every instrument we build begins with a written brief. Not a specification sheet — a conversation that is then committed to paper by hand. The brief records what needs to be measured, to what accuracy, under what conditions, and for how long.

We do not start machining until we understand the use case completely. An instrument designed for laboratory use and one designed for Arctic fieldwork may share the same name but require fundamentally different choices at every subsequent stage.

The brief is kept permanently in the instrument's file. It accompanies the instrument when it leaves the workshop.

→ assets/stage-brief.jpeg
02

Design & Specification

Design at Kesslar & Vane means engineering drawings on vellum, not CAD files. The drawings are made by hand because hand drawing forces precision of thought before precision of manufacture — every line costs effort and cannot be undone without marking the vellum.

Material specifications are determined at this stage: alloy grades, glass types, tolerances at each component interface. The instrument's expected service life — typically 60–100 years — determines which materials are acceptable.

The specification document is reviewed by the client before manufacture begins. Once signed, it becomes contractually binding on both sides.

→ assets/stage-design.jpeg
03

Fabrication & Assembly

All machining is done on the premises. The workshop contains eight lathes, two milling machines, and a grinding room. All are manual. The case for CNC manufacture was considered in 1997 and rejected: the tolerances achievable by a skilled operator on a manual lathe, working slowly, exceed those routinely achievable on automated equipment for small components.

Optical elements — lenses, prisms, graticules — are ground in the optical workshop at the rear of the building. The grinding room is temperature-controlled. It holds a single bench and one worker at a time.

Assembly takes weeks. Every joint is test-fitted before bonding. Every threaded component is lapped to its mating part.

→ assets/stage-fabrication.jpeg
04

Calibration & Documentation

An instrument is not finished until it has been calibrated against a known reference and the results are documented. At Kesslar & Vane, calibration takes longer than fabrication for precision instruments. The theodolites and spectrometers are calibrated over three months minimum.

The calibration certificate records every measurement taken during the process: not just the final result, but the trajectory of adjustments. The certificate is signed by the calibrating technician and by a second technician who checks the work independently.

Documentation is permanent. The instrument receives its number. The file is closed and archived. It can be retrieved at any time — instruments made here in the 1920s still have their files.
