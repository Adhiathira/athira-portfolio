How we work

Survey. Instrument. Chart.

A private practice built on direct field work. No automated vessels. No offshore delegation. Every Sounding Line survey is conducted by our own hydrographers using our own instruments.

The method
01

Bathymetric Survey

We begin with a field survey of the project site. Shallow-water work — harbours, intertidal zones, estuaries — uses traditional line-sounding with a marked lead-line and graduated sounding rod. For deeper or wider survey areas, we deploy a dual-frequency echo sounder on a survey vessel of 5–12m draft.

All survey data is referenced to Chart Datum at the nearest Admiralty reference station. Soundings are corrected for tidal height at the time of measurement, using continuous tide gauge readings throughout the survey period. Raw data is logged in our own field notebooks, which remain part of the project archive.

Accuracy ±0.02m at calm-water datum
Coverage Intertidal to 40m depth
Sounding grid 1m to 10m depending on scale
02

Tidal Study

Where a project requires a tidal study — most harbour engineering work does — we deploy pressure tide gauges at one or more stations around the site. Deployments run for a minimum of two spring-neap cycles (28 days), with longer periods for complex estuary work.

Gauge data is combined with long-term Admiralty tidal prediction tables to produce a site-specific tidal range model. This is the document that coastal engineers use to set cill levels, design quay faces, and specify fendering.

03

Chart Production

Survey data is processed in-house and drawn by hand on a base sheet prepared in our Galway workshop. We do not use automated chart-plotting software. The data is plotted in pencil, corrected, and inked over a working period of two to four weeks depending on survey extent.

Final charts are produced on Sounding Line archival baryta paper — a 160gsm cotton-fibre base manufactured by our partner mill in Fabriano, Italy, to our specification. Charts are hand-annotated with all required metadata: datum, scale bar, soundings key, tidal correction note, and production date. They are supplied rolled in a conservation tube or flat-packed in a portfolio.

On precision

Why hand work still matters.

Automated multi-beam survey vessels produce impressive coverage rates — but coverage is not the same as accuracy. In tidal estuaries, kelp-covered rock shelves, and shallow intertidal zones, automated survey misses detail that a hydrographer on the water, reading conditions and adjusting sounding density in real time, catches by instinct.


The Sounding Line method is slower. A survey that takes an automated vessel one day takes us three. The chart takes six weeks, not six hours. But the chart is right. It does not contain artefacts of beam refraction in shallow turbid water, or false depths produced by kelp returns at 200kHz. A coastal engineer using a Sounding Line chart to set a lock cill level or design a berth face can trust what they are reading.


That is the argument for hand work. Not craft for its own sake. Accuracy for the sake of the structure.
