The Physics of Color Light as architecture

Conventional color requires chemical pigments that absorb light selectively — dyes that bleach, fade, and pollute. IRID takes the opposite approach. Our coatings produce color through constructive interference: nanoscale layers spaced at precise intervals cause specific wavelengths to amplify rather than absorb. The result is a color that cannot fade, because it is not a dye. It is geometry.

This is the same principle that gives butterfly wings and opal gemstones their luminosity. We engineer it at industrial scale — for surfaces that must hold color through decades of UV, temperature cycling, and mechanical stress.

The Process

How thin-film
coating works

Every IRID application follows a four-stage deposition process calibrated to the substrate and target spectral outcome.

01

Substrate Preparation

Surface uniformity determines optical consistency. Substrates are plasma-cleaned at 10⁻⁴ mbar to remove molecular contamination. Surface roughness is characterized by AFM across 5 sample sites — tolerance Ra <0.8nm for optical-grade work.

Plasma & AFM

02

Film Deposition

Alternating high- and low-index layers (TiO₂ / SiO₂) are deposited by reactive magnetron sputtering. Each layer is grown to the target optical thickness in a single chamber pass — deposition rate monitored by in-situ reflectometry.

Magnetron Sputtering

03

Interference Tuning

The spectral peak wavelength is set by controlling cumulative optical path length. Our proprietary interference model calculates the exact layer stack for any target color — from a single-peak narrow-band to a full-spectrum shifting sequence.

Spectral Engineering

04

QA Spectroscopy

Every coated substrate is measured at 15° / 45° / 75° incidence angles using a calibrated spectrophotometer. Pass criteria: peak wavelength ±3nm, reflectance uniformity ≥98% across the coated area. Certificate issued with every shipment.
